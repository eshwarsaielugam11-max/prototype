# Articulatory Precision and Speech Rhythm Changes

Clear articulation depends on the swift, synchronized movement of several anatomical structures within the oral cavity, including the lips, tongue, jaw, and soft palate (velum). In Parkinson's disease, the neuromuscular symptoms of rigidity and bradykinesia impair both the range of motion and the speed of these articulatory organs.

Imprecise articulation in Parkinson's manifests in several distinct acoustic ways:
- **Consonant Imprecision:** Stop consonants (such as /p/, /t/, /k/, /b/, /d/, and /g/) require complete air blockage followed by an explosive burst. When the tongue or lips fail to form a complete seal due to reduced movement amplitude, these sounds lose their crisp boundaries and take on a blurred, spirantized, or slurred acoustic character.
- **Compressed Vowel Space:** During running speech, the acoustic vowel triangle—defined by the formant frequencies $F_1$ and $F_2$ reflecting tongue height and advancement—often becomes centralized and compressed, indicating restricted tongue travel between distinct vowel postures.

In addition to articulation, speech rhythm and timing are characteristically altered:
- **Festinating Speech:** Some speakers experience sudden, involuntary bursts of accelerated speaking rate, often referred to as short rushes of speech. Words may seem to collide with one another as the motor system struggles to regulate pacing.
- **Altered Pause Architecture:** Hesitations and extended silent intervals may occur between words or syllables. Speakers may experience vocal freezing or start-hesitation, finding it difficult to initiate the next acoustic segment.
- **Diadochokinetic Irregularity:** When asked to perform rapid alternating movements—such as repeating syllable sequences like /pa-ta-ka/—speakers with Parkinson's frequently demonstrate progressive degradation in temporal rhythm and amplitude consistency.
