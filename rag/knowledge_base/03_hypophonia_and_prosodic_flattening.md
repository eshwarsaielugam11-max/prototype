# Hypophonia and Monotone Pitch in Parkinsonian Speech

Among the earliest and most prevalent acoustic alterations observed in Parkinson's disease are changes in vocal volume and fundamental frequency modulation, clinically recognized as hypophonia and monopitch.

Hypophonia refers to an involuntary decrease in speech volume, causing speech to sound abnormally soft, quiet, or trailing off toward the end of sentences. This condition stems from physiological changes across multiple speech subsystems:
- **Reduced Subglottal Pressure:** Weakened or less coordinated respiratory muscle drive reduces the air pressure built up below the vocal cords prior to vocalization.
- **Incomplete Glottal Closure:** Rigidity and reduced excursion in the laryngeal musculature prevent the vocal folds from coming fully together, diminishing acoustic resonance and energy transfer.
- **Sensory Processing Mismatch:** A hallmark aspect of hypophonia is an internal sensory calibration deficit. People with Parkinson's frequently perceive their own vocal output as normal or even loud, while conversational partners perceive it as quiet or barely audible. When asked to speak louder, individuals often report feeling as though they are shouting, even though their acoustic output has merely reached normal conversational intensity.

Closely related to hypophonia is prosodic flattening (monopitch and monoloudness). In natural human speech, dynamic shifts in fundamental frequency ($F_0$) and decibel intensity convey emotion, emphasize key concepts, and signal whether a sentence is a statement, question, or exclamation. In Parkinson's disease, the neurological ability to modulate vocal fold length and tension quickly becomes dampened. As a result, speech adopts a uniform, uninflected melody that listeners may mistakenly misinterpret as a lack of engagement, mood flatness, or emotional detachment, even when the speaker experiences vibrant internal affect.
